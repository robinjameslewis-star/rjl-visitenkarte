A process description is useful when people can recognise their actual working day in it. That includes the planned steps, the questions raised along the way and the decisions that have so far been made informally. This picture of the starting point is needed before making changes.

I would begin with a recurring process whose beginning and end can be clearly identified. What triggers the work? What outcome is needed? How can people tell when the task is complete? These questions define the scope of the exercise. Without that boundary, examining one process can quickly turn into describing the entire tax and accounting firm.

## Make steps and handovers visible

Consider a fictional example: documents are needed for a piece of work. A request is prepared, reviewed and sent. Documents arrive, are assigned to the relevant matter and are checked for completeness. Missing information prompts a follow-up. The result is a documented status showing what is available and which next step is possible. This illustrates a possible process; it is not an account of work with a particular firm.

Record a few details for each step. Who takes action? Which information is needed? Where does the work take place? What is passed to the next person? Also note where work stalls or has to be done again. Different perspectives may emerge. What one person considers complete may still be incomplete for the next.

Exceptions are particularly informative. A reply comes through a different channel. A document cannot be confidently assigned to a matter. The person responsible is absent. Include these situations when they are relevant to the process. Record who makes the decision and how another person can take over.

## Review the description together

The process can be mapped through a conversation or prepared using written feedback. A simple table of steps, responsibilities, information and open questions is enough to begin. The people involved then check whether the description is accurate. Unclear points remain visibly open. This prevents an assumed responsibility from accidentally becoming an agreed assignment.

Once this foundation is in place, possible changes can be collected. Perhaps a clear handover is missing. Perhaps the same information has to be entered in several places. Perhaps a task is suited to a fixed rule or support from AI. Each proposal is linked to the problem it is intended to solve. This makes it possible to assess later whether the change actually helps.

Effort and rework should also be recorded using clear definitions. A meaningful comparison later requires a traceable starting point. Estimates can provide initial indications, but they should remain clearly labelled as estimates.
