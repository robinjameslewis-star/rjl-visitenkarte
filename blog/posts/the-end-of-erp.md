In the firm and at the client, I see the same landscape: an inventory management system, an accounting package, a banking tool, a document inbox, payroll, plus spreadsheets. Each system is good on its own. Together they form a patchwork that interfaces have to hold together.

The last piece was about media breaks. An interface removes the media break, but not the transfer: the same data sits in two systems, has to be reconciled and matched, and every hand-over can fail. Outgoing invoices pass through the invoicing interface from inventory management into accounting, payroll entries from the payroll system. These interfaces only exist because two systems within the same company are separate. They are bridges over a ditch that a shared data model would not have.

It is different with the bank statement that arrives via EBICS or a bank API, and with the supplier's e-invoice. Here the interface connects two companies. These bridges remain; what helps here is standards such as EBICS and XRechnung, not a shared system.

In "The end of ERP as we know it?" (11 May 2026), McKinsey describes where this is heading. AI-native solutions, often networks of autonomous agents, operate on top of traditional enterprise applications and orchestrate processes end to end. In the radical scenario, ERP as we know it ceases to exist. McKinsey itself expects something else: the core with its data and business rules remains, but users no longer work in its screens – they work through agents. ERP becomes "headless".

Software providers are building accordingly. On 15 October 2025, Wolters Kluwer launched "CCH Axcess Expert AI" in the US: tax, audit and firm management around a digital core, with AI embedded at the foundation rather than layered onto legacy systems. Pennylane unites accounting, financial management and finance in one platform, from document capture to the tax return; according to the company, over 6,000 firms and over 1,000,000 businesses work with it. Neither does without interfaces: Pennylane lists over 300 integrations, and on 16 September 2026 Wolters Kluwer introduced dedicated access keys for third-party providers. The interface moves to the edge of the platform.

For firms and their clients, this means less reconciliation between their own systems and fewer transfer steps that the procedural documentation has to explain. The shift is not free. Legacy systems, established processes and data migration tie up years; McKinsey expects a hybrid transition, not a break overnight. And a platform that holds everything is a new dependency risk.
