This morning I posted a batch of direct debits. Rent, insurance, leasing – the same amounts, the same payees, the same posting text as last month. And yet I entered every single one by hand. Not because anything needed checking, but because that is how the process works.

Last week's piece argued that mistakes in rules multiply. That does not mean every booking has to be keyed in by hand. Straight-through processing means a transaction travels from the bank feed to the ledger without anyone touching it. The question is not whether that is allowed, but on what terms.

The law does not care who makes the booking. Under *Section 146(1) of the German Fiscal Code (AO)*, entries must be made individually, completely, accurately, promptly and in an orderly way – by hand or by software. To make sure that happens, the GoBD, the German principles for electronic bookkeeping, ask for two things. Under *para. 100*, controls must be put in place, carried out and logged, plausibility checks and reconciliations among them. And where records are generated by software, *para. 111* requires changes to the data and settings that drive them to be recorded too. In other words, a rule must not change without anyone noticing.

As I see it, that tells you when a booking can go through untouched: when it follows a checked rule and nothing about it differs from what the rule expects. Rent for the same amount, to the same payee, with the same reference goes straight through. If the amount goes up, the IBAN changes or the invoice contradicts the rule, the booking stops and lands on someone's desk. Straight-through processing does not do away with checks; it moves them. You check the rule and the exception, not every repetition.

Many firms already have the technology. DATEV's Automatisierungsservice Bank, its automated bank-posting service, classifies bank transactions on the basis of past bookings, uses symbols to show where manual input is needed, and learns from corrections (press release of 10 June 2026). A system that learns is no different from any rule: it is only as good as the bookings it learns from.

The gain is not full automation. It is that time goes where judgement is needed: the rent that has changed, the new supplier, the clash between rule and invoice.

Which of your bookings are the same every month – and what deviation would a system need to spot before you would leave them to it?
