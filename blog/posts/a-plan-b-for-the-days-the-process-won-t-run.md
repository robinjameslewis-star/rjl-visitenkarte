I don't only build the ideal process. I also build the plan B for the days when it doesn't yet run reliably. The reason comes from everyday work in the middle of a changeover: the bank feed is down, an import fails, a list ends up being keyed in by hand after all.

The deadline doesn't wait. In Germany the advance VAT return is due by the tenth day after the end of the period (section 18(1) of the VAT Act, UStG, leaving aside any extension). If all you have by then is the PDF bank statement, you key it in, or you have a plan B.

A plan B does two things. It helps straight away, and it teaches you something. To build a stopgap you have to work out which step is really critical here and what it must deliver at the very least. That brings out the indispensable steps and the room for improvement. Those are the places where I make the overarching automated processes especially robust, with a safety net. And if a new process ever falls over at that point, the fix is already there. In the [workshops](https://robin.vision/workshops/en/) from January 2027 we look for these points together and build a fix for one of them on the spot.

My own example is the Booking Batch Generator, which accountancy firms are asking for just now. It doesn't replace the bank feed. It steps in for the month the feed is missing: PDF statement in, booking batch in DATEV format out, offline in the browser.

There is one condition. The plan B belongs in the procedural documentation. Under Germany's GoBD rules that documentation describes the intended process (paras 151 and 152), and as I read it, that includes what applies on the difficult days. A plan B that only one person knows about is simply the next weak spot. That is the subject of my next piece.
