Goch, the robin on my website, perches on a branch that comes in from the right-hand edge of the picture, as if from a tree. Only I knew that. For a cover image, my AI agent first drew him with no branch at all, then on a branch floating in mid-air. It hadn't broken a rule either time. There was no rule it could have read. Two days later a video tool drew the floating branch again: by then the rule was in a note, just not where the tool would find it.

Harmless enough, it's only a bird. In an accountancy firm such rules matter more. A made-up example: one client's documents always arrive after the 5th, one account is posted differently for them, and only the colleague who has looked after them for years knows why. If she is away, the questions start.

That isn't merely an organisational matter. Under German tax law the books must be able to give an expert third party an overview within a reasonable time (section 145(1) of the Fiscal Code, AO), and the procedural documentation describes the intended process (GoBD, paras 151 and 152). A rule held in one person's head is something no third party can follow.

What only one person knows, nobody can check, nobody can take over and no agent can keep to. This month I have written about rules that can be checked and about having a plan B. Both assume the rule has been written down at all. Automation makes that more pressing. An experienced person fills the gaps with knowledge; an agent fills them with an assumption.

The first step is cheap. For a week, I would write down every answer to the question "How do we do this here?": the rule, the reason, who changed it and when. And I would put it where it is needed, because my video tool showed that a rule in the wrong place is no use. Along the way you get something the next generation can learn from.

Which rule in your firm is known to only one person today?
