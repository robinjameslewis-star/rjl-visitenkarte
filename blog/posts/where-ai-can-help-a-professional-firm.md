A tax and accounting firm wants to use AI. In day-to-day work, follow-up questions accumulate, tasks fall between responsibilities, and new team members have to work out many things for themselves. This provides a concrete starting point: which work should become more reliable, easier to understand or less prone to interruptions?

My approach starts with that question. A useful project first describes a change in how the firm operates. Documents should follow a traceable path. Team members should be able to see what needs to happen next. The firm's leadership should be able to identify where a decision is needed. A clear objective provides the basis for a useful role for automation or AI.

## Understand how the process actually works

Consider a simplified, fictional example. A request arrives. Someone reads it, identifies the relevant matter, asks for missing information and passes it on. A reply comes back later. By then, it may be unclear who is responsible for the next step. Before changing this process with technology, it is worth describing it carefully: what triggers it, what outcome is needed, who is responsible, and what exceptions occur?

I also want to understand the perspective of the people who carry out the process every day. An additional form can give managers a clearer overview while shifting work onto the team. An automatically generated draft can save writing time while requiring more review. Mapping the process therefore includes identifying where the workload should actually be reduced and who will assess that later.

## Prepare a limited trial

A clearly defined part of the process is enough for a first trial. In this example, it might be preparing an internal task overview. First, define which information belongs in it, where that information comes from and when the task is complete. Then consider which steps can follow fixed rules and where AI might help organise information or draft text. Specialist software, a simple checklist or clearer responsibilities may also be part of the solution.

The boundaries need to be equally specific. Which information may the system use? Which outputs require review? What happens when information is missing? Who may initiate the next step? These decisions belong in the process itself. Fictional sample data can be used for an initial demonstration, allowing the team to discuss the approach before real business information is involved.

## Observe what changes

Decide in advance which observations will indicate whether the trial is useful. How often was a follow-up question needed? How much rework was required? Could another person take over the task? Document both the starting point and the observations from the trial. This provides a basis for deciding whether to adjust, extend or stop the trial.

This approach connects technology with people and management. A clear process can support onboarding. Well-documented handovers can make knowledge more accessible. A visible record of progress can make it easier to discuss priorities. Whether these benefits materialise in a particular firm needs to be assessed together.

The first step is manageable: choose a recurring process, describe it with the people involved and record the desired outcome. This can provide the basis for an initial, limited trial.
